When you remove silences, the question isn't whether to cut but how much air to leave. Leave too little and the speaker sounds like a podcast at double speed. Leave too much and the video drags. The right amount depends on the platform, the content and the speaker.

## What the numbers mean

Fluent conversational speech has short gaps between phrases, typically a fifth to a third of a second, and longer ones at the ends of thoughts. Pauses longer than about half a second start to feel like hesitation to a viewer who's ready to swipe away.

Symbaiosis offers three pacings:

| Pacing | Shortens pauses longer than | Gap it leaves | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tight | 0.35 s | 0.12 s | TikTok, Reels and Shorts; energetic delivery |
| Natural | 0.5 s | 0.2 s | YouTube talking heads, vlogs |
| Relaxed | 0.8 s | 0.36 s | tutorials, calm explainers, interviews |

## How to choose

- **Short-form, under a minute:** start with Tight. Viewers expect a fast rhythm and the captions carry them through.
- **Anything you'd watch for more than three minutes:** Natural. Tight pacing becomes tiring over long stretches.
- **Instruction:** Relaxed. People need a moment to follow a step before the next one starts.
- **A slow, deliberate speaker:** one step looser than you'd otherwise pick, so their style survives.

## Pauses that carry meaning

Some pauses do work: the beat before a reveal, the silence after a rhetorical question, a laugh. Automatic tools can't tell those apart from hesitation, which is why every shortened pause appears in the transcript as a ⏸ chip you can tap to keep. Two or three kept pauses in a minute of video usually make the delivery feel intentional rather than mechanical.

## Jump cuts

Shortening pauses in a single-camera video creates jump cuts: the speaker's head moves slightly at each cut. On short-form platforms that's normal and nobody minds. For a smoother look, turn on punch-in zoom, which frames every other section slightly closer so each cut reads as a deliberate camera change.
